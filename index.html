I

The Axial Age

600 – 300 BCE

In the same centuries, in three places that knew nothing of each other, the same questions: what lies behind everything, and how should we live?

1The Axial Age · Türkiye · 6th century BCE

Miletus

Thales, Anaximander, Anaximenes

Key ideaWhat is the first stuff (arche) of all things?

The first school of Western philosophy was born in this harbour city. Thales looked for the first stuff of the world in water and, the story goes, predicted the solar eclipse of 585 BCE. His pupil Anaximander saw the source of all things in the boundless, the "apeiron"; Anaximenes saw it in air. What mattered was not the answers but the question itself: to explain the world by its own principles rather than by the whims of gods.

ThinkIf you were asked today what everything is made of, what would you answer?

2The Axial Age · Türkiye · c. 500 BCE

Ephesus

Heraclitus

Key ideaEverything flows, but the flow has a measure (logos).

Heraclitus said you cannot step into the same river twice: everything changes and opposites turn into one another. Yet the change is not blind; a common measure, a "logos", governs all things, and most people live without noticing it. Ancient writers say he left his book in the temple of Artemis. The single column in the photograph is what remains of that temple.

ThinkIs there anything in your life that never changes?

3The Axial Age · Italy · 5th century BCE

Elea (Velia)

Parmenides, Zeno of Elea

Key ideaWhat is, is; what is not, is not. Could change be an illusion?

Elea was founded around 540 BCE by Phocaeans fleeing the Persians: people from today's Foça, on the Turkish coast. Anatolian philosophy thus crossed to the shore of Italy. In his poem Parmenides urged us to follow the way of reason alone: what is, is, and what is not cannot even be thought; so coming-to-be and passing-away cannot be real. His pupil Zeno questioned even motion, with paradoxes showing that Achilles can never catch the tortoise.

ThinkWhen your eyes and your reason disagree, which do you trust?

4The Axial Age · Greece · 469 – 270 BCE

Athens

Socrates, Plato, Aristotle, Epicurus, Zeno of Citium

Key ideaThe unexamined life is not worth living.

Socrates wrote nothing; he asked people in the agora what they knew, and in 399 BCE he was condemned to death for it. His pupil Plato founded the Academy outside the city; Aristotle classified everything at the Lyceum. Then came Epicurus's Garden and Zeno's Stoa. Almost every branch of Western philosophy, from ethics to logic, from politics to metaphysics, first took shape in this city.

ThinkTake something you are sure you know. How well could you defend it if someone questioned you today?

5The Axial Age · India · c. 5th century BCE

Varanasi and Sarnath

The Buddha; the Upanishadic tradition

Key ideaSuffering has a cause, and the cause can be ended.

Varanasi, on the Ganges, has been a centre of Indian learning for millennia. The Upanishads asked how the self (atman) relates to the ground of the universe (brahman). After his awakening the Buddha gave his first teaching just outside the city, in the Deer Park at Sarnath: the Four Noble Truths and the middle way between two extremes. A teaching that also questioned any lasting "self", it spread across most of Asia.

ThinkWhat are you most tired of clinging to?

6The Axial Age · China · 6th – 4th century BCE (tradition)

Louguantai

Laozi; the Daodejing

Key ideaThe way that can be named is not the constant Way (dao).

Daoist tradition places here the observatory of Yin Xi, keeper of the pass. The legend says it was Yin Xi who asked Laozi, travelling west to withdraw from the world, to write down his teaching: the result was the Daodejing. Whether Laozi lived is debated, and the text was probably compiled in the 4th–3rd centuries BCE. But the idea is strong: what is soft like water overcomes the hard, and acting without forcing (wu wei) is the best way to govern.

ThinkIs there something this week that would settle by itself if you stopped forcing it?

7The Axial Age · China · 551 – 479 BCE

Qufu

Confucius (Kongzi)

Key ideaWe become fully human by living our relationships and rituals with care (ren).

Confucius lived and taught in Qufu, capital of the state of Lu. His sayings were gathered by his students in the Analects (Lunyu). For him a good society rests not on fear of laws but on the virtue of its people: humaneness (ren), right conduct and ritual (li), and the exemplary person (junzi). His temple, cemetery and the Kong family mansion are a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

ThinkWho first taught you how to treat other people well?

II

Schools and empires

300 BCE – 600 CE

Philosophy settles into libraries, schools and imperial capitals; Greek, Roman, Christian and Buddhist thought become institutions.

8Schools and empires · Egypt · 3rd century BCE – 415 CE

Alexandria

Euclid, Philo, Plotinus, Hypatia

Key ideaCan all knowledge be gathered in one place?

The Ptolemies founded the Mouseion here and the most famous library of the ancient world. Euclid set geometry in order here; the Jewish philosopher Philo read Moses through Plato. Plotinus, founder of Neoplatonism, studied here with Ammonius Saccas for eleven years before going to Rome. Hypatia, mathematician and Platonist philosopher, was killed by a mob in 415. The Bibliotheca Alexandrina in the photograph opened in 2002 in memory of the ancient library.

ThinkWhen a library burns, what is really lost?

  • SEP: Plotinus
9Schools and empires · Italy · 1st century BCE – 180 CE

Rome

Cicero, Seneca, Epictetus, Marcus Aurelius

Key ideaTelling apart what is up to us and what is not: Stoic freedom.

Cicero gave Greek philosophy a Latin vocabulary. In his Letters to Lucilius Seneca wrote about anger, time and death. Epictetus, born a slave in Hierapolis, taught in Rome until the emperor banished the philosophers from the city. Marcus Aurelius wrote notes to himself, in Greek, in his campaign tent: the book we now read as the Meditations. His bronze equestrian statue in the photograph is in the Capitoline Museums.

ThinkOf the things troubling you today, which are really up to you?

10Schools and empires · Algeria · 354 – 430

Hippo (Annaba)

Augustine

Key ideaWhat is time? If no one asks me, I know; if I wish to explain it, I do not.

Augustine was born at Thagaste in today's Algeria, studied in Carthage and Milan, then returned to Hippo as bishop and stayed until his death. In the Confessions he told his own inner life as a philosophical journey and argued that time is a "distension" of the soul. In the City of God he thought about history and politics. He died while the city was under Vandal siege. The basilica in the photograph was built in the 19th century on the hill above the ruins of ancient Hippo.

ThinkThe past no longer exists and the future does not yet. So how long is now?

  • SEP: Saint Augustine
11Schools and empires · India · 5th – 12th century

Nalanda

The tradition of Nagarjuna; Xuanzang

Key ideaNothing has an independent essence of its own (shunyata, emptiness).

For seven hundred years Nalanda was one of the greatest centres of learning in the world; the Chinese monk Xuanzang studied here for years in the 7th century. Tradition links Nagarjuna, founder of the Madhyamaka school, with Nalanda too, although he probably lived earlier, in the 2nd century. His idea was bold: everything exists in dependence on other things, so nothing has an essence of its own. The ruins joined the UNESCO list in 2016.

ThinkIf you set yourself apart from all other people and circumstances, what would be left?

III

Houses of wisdom

800 – 1400

Greek philosophy is translated into Arabic, rethought from Baghdad to Córdoba, and passed on from there to Latin Europe.

12Houses of wisdom · Iraq · 8th – 10th century

Baghdad

Hunayn ibn Ishaq, al-Kindi, al-Farabi

Key ideaWe should not be ashamed to take the truth, wherever it comes from.

In the Abbasid capital, around the House of Wisdom, a translation movement that lasted two centuries brought Aristotle, Plato and Galen into Arabic, much of it through Syriac and the heritage of the schools of Nisibis and Edessa. Al-Kindi, "the philosopher of the Arabs", wrote that we should accept the truth whoever says it. Al-Farabi, called "the second teacher" after Aristotle, thought about what a virtuous city should be. The Mustansiriya Madrasa in the photograph (1227–34) is one of the oldest buildings of learning still standing in Baghdad.

ThinkWhen did you last learn something true from someone you did not like?

13Houses of wisdom · Uzbekistan · 980 – 1037

Bukhara

Ibn Sina (Avicenna)

Key ideaThe floating man: even perceiving nothing of your body, you would know that you exist.

Ibn Sina was born near Bukhara; as a young man he cured the Samanid ruler and in return was allowed into the royal library. In The Cure and the Canon of Medicine he gathered logic, nature, the soul and medicine into one system. In a famous thought experiment he argued that a man created in empty space, perceiving nothing, would still know that he exists. His distinction between existence and essence became a basic idea of the Latin Middle Ages. The Samanid Mausoleum in the photograph was already standing in his day.

ThinkIf you could see, hear and touch nothing, would you still say "I"?

14Houses of wisdom · Spain · 1126 – 1204

Córdoba

Ibn Rushd (Averroes), Moses Maimonides

Key ideaReason and faith do not conflict; truth does not contradict truth.

Two great thinkers were born in the same city in the same generation. Ibn Rushd, a judge of Córdoba, wrote such commentaries on Aristotle that the Latin world called him simply "the Commentator"; against al-Ghazali he defended philosophy in The Incoherence of the Incoherence. Maimonides was forced to leave the city with his family and wrote The Guide for the Perplexed in Cairo. The arches of the Great Mosque in the photograph saw the childhood of both.

ThinkWhen something you believe and what your reason tells you collide, what do you do?

15Houses of wisdom · Türkiye · 1207 – 1273

Konya

Jalal al-Din Rumi

Key ideaThe reed cries because it was torn from the reed bed: separation and return.

Born in Balkh, Rumi moved with his family away from the Mongol threat and settled in Konya. His meeting with Shams of Tabriz in 1244 turned a scholar of law and religion into a poet. The Masnavi opens with the complaint of the reed flute: everything torn from its origin longs to return. Not a philosophical system but a way of knowing through love, and a voice that still joins East and West today.

ThinkWhere is your own "reed bed", the place you long to return to?

16Houses of wisdom · France · 1100 – 1277

Paris

Peter Abelard, Thomas Aquinas

Key ideaFaith can seek understanding through reason; Aristotle meets Christianity.

Abelard taught in Paris early in the 12th century; in Sic et Non he set conflicting authorities side by side to make his students think. Around 1200 the University of Paris was born. Aristotle, read anew thanks to the commentaries of Ibn Rushd, caused fierce debate here; Thomas Aquinas came twice as a master and tried in the Summa Theologiae to reconcile reason and faith. The photograph shows the Sorbonne, the college founded in 1257, as it stands today.

ThinkCould you write the strongest objection to your own view?

17Houses of wisdom · Tunisia · 1332 – 1406

Tunis

Ibn Khaldun

Key ideaSocieties are built by asabiyya (solidarity) and fall when they lose it.

Ibn Khaldun was born in Tunis, served as a statesman in the courts of North Africa and al-Andalus, then withdrew to a fortress and in 1377 wrote the Muqaddimah. He treated history not as a mere sequence of events but as a science with causes: the strong solidarity (asabiyya) of nomadic groups carries them to power, and the comforts of city life dissolve that bond within a few generations. He is seen today as a forerunner of sociology and of the philosophy of history. His statue in the photograph stands on the main avenue of Tunis.

ThinkWhat holds the people around you together?

IV

Rebirths

1400 – 1700

In Florence, Timbuktu, Isfahan, Aksum and Mexico, reason speaks again, and in new forms.

18Rebirths · Italy · 1438 – 1499

Florence

Plethon, Marsilio Ficino, Pico della Mirandola

Key ideaThe human being is the creature that can choose its own place.

Plethon came from Constantinople to the Council of Florence in 1438–39 and his talks on Plato impressed the city's ruler, Cosimo de' Medici. Cosimo later gave the young Marsilio Ficino a villa at Careggi, and Ficino translated all of Plato into Latin. In his Oration on the Dignity of Man Pico della Mirandola argued that the human being has no fixed place and can shape itself. This was the intellectual heart of the Renaissance.

ThinkIn which direction would you like to shape yourself today?

19Rebirths · Mali · 15th – 16th century

Timbuktu

Ahmad Baba and the scholars of Sankore

Key ideaKnowledge lives in books and in the people who protect them.

In the 15th and 16th centuries this city on the edge of the Sahara was a great centre of learning around the Sankore, Djinguereber and Sidi Yahya mosques: law, logic, astronomy, ethics. Its most famous scholar, Ahmad Baba (1556–1627), was exiled to Marrakesh after the Moroccan conquest of 1591 and wrote of losing his books. Families of the city kept hundreds of thousands of manuscripts for generations; during the occupation of 2012–13 many were smuggled out and saved.

ThinkWhich book would you save?

20Rebirths · Iran · 1598 – 1640

Isfahan

Mir Damad, Mulla Sadra

Key ideaExistence comes before essence, and everything is in constant motion at its core.

In the Safavid capital the "School of Isfahan" brought together the philosophy of Ibn Sina, Suhrawardi's illuminationism and Sufism. Mulla Sadra, a pupil of Mir Damad, built his "transcendent philosophy": what is real is existence, and essences are its limits; and everything changes not only in place and quality but in its very substance (substantial motion). The line that has kept philosophy alive in Iran down to today runs through here. Naqsh-e Jahan Square in the photograph was laid out in the same years.

ThinkAre you really someone different today from who you were yesterday?

21Rebirths · Ethiopia · 1599 – 1692

Aksum

Zera Yacob

Key ideaReason is a light given to test the claims of every faith.

Zera Yacob was born near Aksum. During a time of religious persecution he hid in a cave for two years and there, by his own account, decided not to believe anything merely because he had been taught it. His Hatata ("Inquiry"), written in 1667, treats reason as a light given by God to test the claims of all religions, and argues for the equality of women and men. Its authorship has been debated since the 1920s; many scholars today defend it as a genuine 17th-century Ethiopian work.

ThinkIs there something you believe only because you were taught it?

22Rebirths · Mexico · 1669 – 1695

Mexico City

Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz

Key ideaThe right of women to learn: thinking belongs to everyone's nature.

In the capital of New Spain Sor Juana entered the convent of San Jerónimo and turned her cell into a great library, an observatory and a literary salon. When a bishop urged her to leave worldly subjects alone, she wrote her Reply to Sor Filotea in 1691: one of the first great defences in the Americas of women's right to learn and to think. Her long poem First Dream follows the soul on a night journey trying to understand the whole universe. The convent is now a university that bears her name.

ThinkHave you ever asked a question you were told was "not your business"?

23Rebirths · Netherlands · 1632 – 1677

Amsterdam and Rijnsburg

Spinoza (Descartes also lived in the Dutch Republic)

Key ideaGod or Nature: one substance, infinite expressions.

Spinoza was born into Amsterdam's Sephardic Jewish community and was expelled from it in 1656, at 23. He lived by grinding lenses, and in 1661–63 he lived in this small house in Rijnsburg, near Leiden. He built the Ethics like a geometry book, with definitions and propositions: God and Nature are one and the same, and freedom is understanding the causes that determine us. The book could only be published after his death. Descartes too had lived about twenty years in the Dutch Republic and printed his Discourse on Method in Leiden.

ThinkDoes understanding the causes of your anger make it smaller?

V

Modern questions

1700 – 1950

How far can knowledge reach, what is freedom, how should the individual live? The great modern questions, from Edinburgh to Kyoto.

24Modern questions · Scotland · 1711 – 1790

Edinburgh

David Hume, Adam Smith

Key ideaWe cannot prove by reason that the sun will rise tomorrow; we expect it by habit.

Hume stood at the centre of the Scottish Enlightenment. In A Treatise of Human Nature he argued that our idea of causation comes not from observation but from habit: we cannot show by logic that the future will be like the past just because it always has been. He also noticed that an "ought" cannot be derived from an "is". His close friend Adam Smith wrote The Theory of Moral Sentiments and then The Wealth of Nations. Students touch the big toe of his statue on the Royal Mile for luck.

ThinkHow much of what you are sure of is really habit?

25Modern questions · Russia · 1724 – 1804

Königsberg (Kaliningrad)

Immanuel Kant

Key ideaDare to know! Have the courage to use your own understanding.

Kant spent his whole life in Königsberg and hardly ever left it; even so, he moved philosophy from its place. In the Critique of Pure Reason (1781) he showed that the way we know the world depends on the structure of our minds. In ethics he set out the categorical imperative: act so that your principle could be a law for everyone, and never treat a person merely as a means. His answer to "What is Enlightenment?": Sapere aude. His tomb is on the wall of the cathedral.

ThinkIf everyone did one thing you did today, what would the world be like?

26Modern questions · Germany · 1810 – 1841

Berlin

Fichte, Hegel, the young Marx

Key ideaHistory is the progress of the consciousness of freedom; philosophers have interpreted the world, the point is to change it.

At the University of Berlin, founded in 1810, Fichte was the first elected rector; Hegel taught here from 1818 until his death in 1831. For him reality was a process that develops through contradictions, and history the progress of the consciousness of freedom. The young Karl Marx came in 1836 to study law and joined the Young Hegelians; later he would try to set Hegel "on his feet". Marx's eleventh thesis on Feuerbach is written in the entrance hall of the main building.

ThinkWhich is harder: understanding an idea, or living it?

27Modern questions · Denmark · 1813 – 1855

Copenhagen

Søren Kierkegaard

Key ideaLife can only be understood backwards, but it must be lived forwards.

Kierkegaard spent almost his whole life in Copenhagen and wrote most of his books under pseudonyms. In Either/Or and Fear and Trembling he argued that the great systems forget the anxiety, choice and faith of the single individual: the truth must be a truth for me, one I can live for. That is why he is seen as a forerunner of existentialism. His statue in the photograph stands in the garden of the Royal Library.

ThinkIs there a truth you could live for?

28Modern questions · USA · 1836 – 1854

Concord and Walden Pond

Ralph Waldo Emerson, Henry David Thoreau

Key ideaTrust yourself; and do not submit to an unjust law.

In this small Massachusetts town Emerson published Nature in 1836 and became the voice of American Transcendentalism: each person should trust the voice within. His young friend Thoreau lived from 1845 to 1847 in a cabin he built on Emerson's land beside Walden Pond, and wrote that he went to the woods "to front only the essential facts of life". His essay on civil disobedience later influenced Gandhi and Martin Luther King.

ThinkWhat could you let go of that your life does not really need?

29Modern questions · Japan · 1911 – 1945

Kyoto

Nishida Kitarō and the Kyoto School

Key ideaPure experience: the moment before subject and object are divided.

In 1911 Nishida Kitarō published An Inquiry into the Good and started the Kyoto School, which thought through Zen experience in the language of Western philosophy. For him, in the purest moment of experience there is not yet a split between "I" and "world"; in later work he developed the ideas of "place" (basho) and "absolute nothingness". Because he walked and thought every day along the canal leading to Ginkaku-ji, the path is now called the Philosopher's Path (Tetsugaku no michi). The route ends here, with an invitation to think while walking.

ThinkWhen were you last so absorbed in something that you forgot yourself?
