Key ideas

  1. A unified Aristotle

    He read Aristotle’s philosophy as one coherent system. Where Aristotle is unclear, he lists possible readings instead of charging him with contradiction. SEP §2 ↗

  2. Metaphysics as a science

    He argued that metaphysics can be a demonstrative science with its own subject and axioms, chief among them the principle of non-contradiction. SEP §4 ↗

  3. Passive and active intellect

    The human intellect is receptive; the active intellect is not part of the soul but transcendent and divine. SEP §5 ↗

  4. Fate and free action

    In “On Fate” he reads fate as the natural constitution of things, leaving room for chance. He argues against the Stoic reconciliation of fate and freedom. SEP §6 ↗

Listen

Aphrodisias was a Roman city famous for its sculpture workshops. Alexander lived here in the late 2nd and early 3rd century AD and became the most influential commentator on Aristotle. An inscription found here in 2001 names him as one of the heads of the philosophical schools in Athens. In his treatise On Fate he asked how people could be held responsible if everything were fixed in advance, and argued against the Stoic view of fate.

Think

If everything were fixed in advance, would it be fair to blame you for what you do?

Timeline

  1. Late 2nd c.

    Begins his career; his teachers include Aristoteles of Mytilene and Sosigenes.

  2. 198–209

    Dedicated “On Fate” to the co-emperors Septimius Severus and Caracalla.

  3. 2001

    An inscription found at Aphrodisias names him head of a philosophical school in Athens.

What we don't know

The order of his works is unknown, and some works are wrongly attributed to him. Which chair he held in Athens is unclear.

Legacy

In late antiquity and the Arabic world he was known simply as “the Commentator”; Maimonides drew on him.
