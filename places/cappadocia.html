In the 4th century three thinkers from this region brought Greek philosophy into dialogue with Christian thought. Kayseri (Caesarea) was Basil's city.

Who: The Cappadocian Fathers: Basil, Gregory of Nyssa, Gregory of Nazianzus

Key ideas

  1. An incomprehensible God

    For Gregory of Nyssa, God is incomprehensible; trying to define God completely is presumptuous in the extreme. IEP §2 ↗

  2. The image of God

    The fundamental fact about human nature is that humans are created in the image of God: reason and free choice are its marks. IEP §4 ↗

  3. Unending perfection

    For Gregory the process of perfection is unending: moral and intellectual growth has no final stop. IEP §5 ↗

  4. Using pagan wisdom

    Basil urged young people to read the Greek poets and philosophers, choosing what is useful for virtue. Britannica ↗

Listen

In the 4th century three thinkers came from Cappadocia: Basil of Caesarea, his brother Gregory of Nyssa, and their friend Gregory of Nazianzus. All three were trained in Greek philosophy. Basil urged young people to take what was good from pagan poets and philosophers. Gregory of Nyssa argued that God is incomprehensible and that humans are made in the image of God; for him, perfection was a process without end. The rock churches of Göreme, from later centuries, are where these ideas were told in pictures.

Think

Does growth without end feel tiring to you, or freeing?

Timeline

  1. 329

    Basil born in Caesarea (Kayseri).

  2. c. 335

    His brother Gregory born in Cappadocia.

  3. 370

    Basil became bishop of Caesarea.

  4. 379

    Basil died; Gregory of Nazianzus moved to Constantinople.

  5. c. 395

    Gregory of Nyssa died.

What we don't know

The rock churches of Göreme were made centuries after the Cappadocian Fathers; the link between them is the region's continuing religious and intellectual tradition, not a direct trace.

Legacy

The Cappadocian Fathers were decisive in thinking Christian theology in the language of Greek philosophy; Gregory's idea of endless growth is being reread in modern theology.
