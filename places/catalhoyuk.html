Çatalhöyük

Konya (Çumra) · 7100–5950 BC

On this mound in the Konya Plain thousands of people lived for over a thousand years in houses packed wall to wall, with no streets. No palace or temple has been found, and the houses are much alike. The dead were buried beneath the floors and the walls were painted. Before philosophy, it shows people seeking equality, home and meaning; it is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Who: The Neolithic townspeople of the Konya Plain

Key ideas

  1. A town without a palace

    The houses are very alike in size and layout; no palace, temple or elite quarter has been found so far. Recent studies suggest that women and men ate alike and held similar standing. Yet grinding stones and storage were not shared out equally, and equality may have faded over time. Wikipedia ↗

  2. The dead beneath the floor

    The dead were buried under the floors, hearths and platforms of the houses. The living slept and cooked above their ancestors. A house was shelter and memory at once; when one was pulled down, the new one rose in the same place, on the same plan. Wikipedia ↗

  3. The first landscape?

    One wall painting seems to show a settlement with the twin peaks of Mount Hasan rising behind it, and has been called “the world’s oldest map” or “the first landscape painting”. Some scholars read it as the pattern of a leopard skin. What do we read an image by? Wikipedia ↗

  4. The mother goddess debate

    Its first excavator, James Mellaart, took the figurines as evidence of a mother goddess cult. Ian Hodder’s team looked again at the same finds and questioned that picture. How much of our own questions we load onto a silent past is archaeology’s philosophical question. Wikipedia ↗

Çatalhöyük has no writing; the ideas here are archaeologists’ interpretations, and those interpretations are themselves debated. The section shows how questions philosophy would later ask were lived out in a town.

Art and monuments

  • Wall paintingsHunting scenes, people around a giant bull, and hand prints; many are in the Museum of Anatolian Civilizations in Ankara.
  • The Seated WomanA clay figurine seated between feline-headed armrests, c. 6000 BC.
  • Horned benchesHorns of wild cattle set into walls and platforms.

A walk on site

  1. The North Area Under the shelter roof, layers of houses built one on top of another.
  2. The South Area A deep trench down into the oldest layers of the mound.
  3. The experimental house A house rebuilt in mudbrick, with its ladder through the roof and plastered walls.

Listen

Nine thousand years ago there were no streets here. Houses leaned on each other like a honeycomb; people walked across the rooftops and climbed down into their homes by a ladder through a hole in the roof. Inside were a hearth, plastered platforms, bulls’ horns and red paintings on the walls. The dead lay beneath those platforms. Archaeologists looked here for a king’s palace and found none: the houses are alike. Perhaps that shows people could live long as equals; perhaps we are reading our own hopes into the past. Standing on the mound, look across the plain to distant Mount Hasan and ask: what makes a place a home?

Think

What ties you to the place you live: its walls, its people or its memories?

Timeline

  1. c. 7100 BC

    First settlement on the mound.

  2. c. 6700–6500 BC

    The town at its largest: perhaps 5,000 to 7,000 people.

  3. c. 6000 BC

    The Seated Woman figurine is made.

  4. c. 5950 BC

    The East Mound is abandoned.

  5. 1958–1965

    Discovered and excavated by James Mellaart.

  6. 1993–2017

    New excavations under Ian Hodder.

  7. 2012

    Inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

What we don't know

We do not know why people lived in streetless houses entered through the roof, what the wall paintings meant, or why the town was abandoned. The egalitarian reading is itself debated.

Legacy

Çatalhöyük is one of the best studied examples of the move to settled life and the birth of the town. The excavation itself became a laboratory for “reflexive archaeology”, which asks how far we can know a past we interpret.
