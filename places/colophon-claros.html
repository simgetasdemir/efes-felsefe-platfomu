Colophon and Claros

İzmir (Menderes) · 6th–5th c. BC

Xenophanes (c. 570–475 BC) was the first thinker to attack the habit of picturing the gods as human beings. When the Persians took Ionia he left Colophon, aged about twenty-five, and by his own account wandered the Greek world for sixty-seven years. In the city’s territory lay Claros, one of the great oracles of Apollo.

Who: Xenophanes of Colophon

Key ideas

  1. Gods in our own image

    Ethiopians picture their gods as black and snub-nosed, Thracians as blue-eyed and red-haired; and if horses and oxen had hands, says Xenophanes, they would draw gods shaped like horses and oxen. Our pictures of the divine tell us more about ourselves than about the gods. SEP §2 ↗

  2. One god, not like mortals

    Against Homer and Hesiod, who gave the gods theft, adultery and deceit, Xenophanes speaks of “one god greatest among gods and men, not at all like mortals in body or in thought”. This god stays in one place and shakes all things by the thought of his mind. SEP §3 ↗

  3. Wisdom over victory

    Cities honour Olympic champions with feasts and front seats, yet their strength does not make a city better governed. For Xenophanes “our wisdom” is worth more than the strength of men and horses. Philosophy becomes a claim about what a community ought to value. SEP §4 ↗

  4. Nature without myths

    Iris, the rainbow goddess, is for him “a cloud, purple, red and yellow-green to see”. Shells and the imprints of fish in the rocks show that the land was once under the sea. Where others saw gods, he looked for natural causes. SEP §5 ↗

  5. What can we know?

    “The clear and certain truth no man has seen”: even someone who said it exactly would not know it, and opinion lies over everything. Yet the gods did not reveal all things at the start; by searching, people find out better in time. Doubt and inquiry are born together. SEP §6 ↗

Listen

In the sixth century BC Colophon was one of the rich cities of Ionia, famous for its cavalry and its luxury. Xenophanes was born here, left when the Persians came, and spent his life on the road, reciting his poems. Everywhere he went he saw the same thing: every people made its gods in its own likeness. Homer’s gods stole, lied and envied like human beings. Xenophanes thought this a disgrace. At Claros, the city’s oracle, people travelled far to ask Apollo about the future. Xenophanes proposed something else: the gods did not show us everything from the start; by searching, we find out better in time. Standing by the oracle’s temple, ask yourself: do you wait for an answer from a god, or go looking for it?

Think

When you picture what you hold highest, whose face does it wear?

Timeline

  1. c. 570 BC

    Born in Colophon.

  2. 546/545 BC

    The Persians under Harpagus take Ionia; at about 25 Xenophanes leaves home.

  3. c. 545–475 BC

    By his own poem, sixty-seven years of wandering the Greek world, reciting his verse (B8).

  4. Later

    Plato and Aristotle link him, perhaps loosely, to Parmenides and the Eleatic school.

What we don't know

Only about forty short fragments survive. We do not know whether he wrote a work “On Nature”, and whether his “one god” amounts to strict monotheism is still debated.

Legacy

Heraclitus of Ephesus named him among those whose much learning did not teach understanding; yet his critique of gods made in human form runs from Plato’s Republic through the theology of the philosophers to modern debates about religion as projection.
