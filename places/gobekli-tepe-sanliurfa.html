Şanlıurfa and Harran

Şanlıurfa · 9600 BC – 10th c. AD

At Göbekli Tepe people raised monumental structures more than 11,000 years ago. The school of Edessa, Urfa's ancient name, translated Greek works into Syriac; Thabit ibn Qurra, born in Harran, carried Greek mathematics into Arabic.

Key ideas

  1. Monuments before writing

    The monumental structures of Göbekli Tepe were raised by hunter-gatherers between 9600 and 8200 BC, most likely for rituals. UNESCO ↗

  2. The school of Edessa

    In the schools of Edessa (Urfa) and Nisibis, Greek works were translated into Syriac in the 4th and 5th centuries, and later Aristotle's logic too. SEP §1 ↗

  3. A translator from Harran

    Thabit ibn Qurra, a Sabian from Harran, translated and revised many important Greek works, including Euclid's Elements. MacTutor ↗

  4. Amicable numbers

    Thabit found a rule that produces pairs of amicable numbers; he was probably the first to find the pair 17296 and 18416. MacTutor ↗

Listen

At Göbekli Tepe, hunter-gatherers raised monumental structures between 9600 and 8200 BC, with T-shaped pillars carved with wild animals. Thousands of years later Urfa, ancient Edessa, was home to one of the schools where Greek works were translated into Syriac. In nearby Harran, Thabit ibn Qurra, born in 836, revised translations of Greek mathematicians such as Euclid in Baghdad and found a rule for amicable numbers. Harran is often called "the first university"; the sources we checked do not confirm that, but it was a crossroads on the road of learning.

Think

Before writing, what did people raise stones to say? What would you carve in stone today?

What we don't know

Harran is often called "the world's first university". The sources we checked (Britannica, SEP, MacTutor) do not confirm this; that Harran was a crossroads of learning and translation is clear.

Legacy

Without the Syriac and Arabic translations, much of Greek science and philosophy would never have reached medieval Europe.
