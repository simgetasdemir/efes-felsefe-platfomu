For a thousand years the Byzantine capital, Constantinople, kept ancient Greek philosophy alive by teaching and copying it. Psellos revived Plato here; Plethon was born here.

Who: Michael Psellos, Gemistos Plethon

Key ideas

  1. Education and transmission

    Byzantine higher education kept teaching ancient Greek philosophy. Most of the Greek philosophical texts we read today reached us through Byzantine manuscripts. SEP §1.1 ↗

  2. Commentary and compendium

    Byzantine philosophy was often written as commentary, paraphrase and textbook; originality lay in rearranging and interpreting older texts. SEP §1.2 ↗

  3. Psellos and Plethon

    Michael Psellos (1018–1078) revived interest in Plato and the Neoplatonists. Gemistos Plethon (c. 1360–1452) was among the most influential carriers of Platonism to the West. SEP §1.3 ↗

  4. Soul and universals

    The immortality of the soul and the existence of universals were two great debates in which Byzantine thinkers sought a path between Plato and Aristotle. SEP §2 ↗

Listen

Constantinople became the new capital of the Roman Empire in 330 and stayed so for more than a thousand years. Here Plato and Aristotle were taught, commented on and copied; most of the Greek philosophical texts we read today reached us through Byzantine manuscripts. In the 11th century Michael Psellos, with the title "consul of the philosophers", revived interest in Plato and the Neoplatonists. Gemistos Plethon was born here, and in 1438–39 he went to Italy and helped carry Platonism into the Renaissance. Stand under the dome of Hagia Sophia: Procopius said it seemed suspended from Heaven.

What we don't know

Whether Byzantine philosophy was original has long been debated. The SEP entry stresses that the answer depends largely on what we mean by "originality" and "philosophy".

Legacy

Without the manuscripts preserved in Byzantium, much of Plato and Aristotle would be lost. Byzantine scholars who went to Italy in the 15th century fed Renaissance Platonism.
