Knidos

Muğla (Datça) · 4th c. BC

Eudoxus (c. 390–340 BC), who worked at Plato’s Academy, was one of the greatest mathematicians of antiquity: the theory of proportion, the method of exhaustion and the model of nested spheres for the planets are his. He went home to Knidos and built an observatory. Praxiteles’ famous Aphrodite also stood in this city.

Who: Eudoxus of Cnidus

Key ideas

  1. The precision of ratio

    Eudoxus found a way to compare even ratios of magnitudes that have no common measure. Book V of Euclid’s Elements rests largely on his work, and his theory inspired Dedekind’s definition of the real numbers in the nineteenth century. MacTutor ↗

  2. The method of exhaustion

    To pin down the area of a curved figure, fill it with polygons of more and more sides: Eudoxus made this into a rigorous method of proof. Archimedes used it, and it is a distant ancestor of the integral calculus. Wikipedia ↗

  3. Saving the appearances

    The planets seem to wander back and forth across the sky. Eudoxus explained each with nested spheres turning on different axes. Finding regular causes for a motion that looks irregular, it was astronomy’s first great theoretical model, and Aristotle built his cosmology on it. Wikipedia ↗

  4. Is pleasure the good?

    According to Aristotle, Eudoxus held that pleasure is the good, since all creatures aim at it. Aristotle adds that his arguments were credited more for the excellence of his character than for their own sake, since he was a temperate man. MIT Classics NE X.2 ↗

There is no SEP entry for Eudoxus, and none of his works survives. This section draws on the MacTutor history of mathematics archive, Euclid’s Elements and Aristotle’s report.

Art and monuments

  • The Aphrodite of KnidosPraxiteles’ statue of the 4th century BC, the first great female nude; the original is lost, Roman copies survive.
  • The Lion of KnidosA giant marble lion from a monumental tomb near the city, now in the British Museum.
  • Theatre and two harboursA Hellenistic theatre just above the commercial harbour.

A walk on site

  1. The two harbours and the theatre Two harbours on either side of the narrow neck joining the mainland to the cape.
  2. The Temple of Aphrodite The terrace of the round temple where Praxiteles’ statue is thought to have stood.
  3. Cape Deveboynu The cape where the Aegean meets the Mediterranean, with its lighthouse.

Listen

Knidos lies at the tip of the Datça peninsula, between two seas. The ancient city built two harbours here and grew famous for its merchants, sailors and physicians. Eudoxus left for Athens and Plato’s circle; after changing mathematics and astronomy he came home and built an observatory to watch the night sky. For him the wandering paths of the planets came from the regular turning of invisible spheres. In the same city stood Praxiteles’ Aphrodite; ancient writers tell of ships calling at Knidos just to see it. Sitting on the cape at dusk, look up and ask: is looking for order behind what seems irregular a habit, or a faith?

Think

What seems irregular in your life, and what order are you looking for behind it?

Timeline

  1. c. 390 BC

    Born in Knidos.

  2. Youth

    Studies mathematics with Archytas of Tarentum, hears Plato in Athens, observes the sky in Egypt.

  3. c. 368 BC

    Returns to Athens with his pupils and works closely with Plato’s Academy.

  4. Later

    Goes home to Knidos, serves in the city assembly and builds an observatory.

  5. c. 340 BC

    Dies.

What we don't know

None of his works survives; we know him through Euclid, Aristotle, Hipparchus and later commentators. How his spheres actually worked has largely been reconstructed by modern scholars.

Legacy

Some count him the greatest mathematician of classical Greece. His theory of proportion was taught for two thousand years through the Elements, and the idea of nested spheres shaped the medieval picture of the cosmos through Aristotle and Ptolemy.

Connections

  • Assos: Aristotle discussed Eudoxus’ hedonism and built his cosmology on Eudoxus’ nested spheres.
  • İznik: Hipparchus of Nicaea wrote a commentary criticising Eudoxus’ description of the sky; fragments of Eudoxus survive in it.
