ÇATALHÖYÜK · 7100 BC

Walk through Çatalhöyük

The Neolithic townspeople of the Konya Plain · 7100–5950 BC · Konya (Çumra)

On this mound in the Konya Plain thousands of people lived for over a thousand years in houses packed wall to wall, with no streets. No palace or temple has been found, and the houses are much alike. The dead were buried beneath the floors and the walls were painted. Before philosophy, it shows people seeking equality, home and meaning; it is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

The North Area

Under the shelter roof, layers of houses built one on top of another.

IdeaA town without a palace

The houses are very alike in size and layout; no palace, temple or elite quarter has been found so far. Recent studies suggest that women and men ate alike and held similar standing. Yet grinding stones and storage were not shared out equally, and equality may have faded over time. Wikipedia ↗

The South Area

A deep trench down into the oldest layers of the mound.

IdeaThe dead beneath the floor

The dead were buried under the floors, hearths and platforms of the houses. The living slept and cooked above their ancestors. A house was shelter and memory at once; when one was pulled down, the new one rose in the same place, on the same plan. Wikipedia ↗

The experimental house

A house rebuilt in mudbrick, with its ladder through the roof and plastered walls.

IdeaThe first landscape?

One wall painting seems to show a settlement with the twin peaks of Mount Hasan rising behind it, and has been called “the world’s oldest map” or “the first landscape painting”. Some scholars read it as the pattern of a leopard skin. What do we read an image by? Wikipedia ↗

Before you leave

Sit for a moment

Nine thousand years ago there were no streets here. Houses leaned on each other like a honeycomb; people walked across the rooftops and climbed down into their homes by a ladder through a hole in the roof. Inside were a hearth, plastered platforms, bulls’ horns and red paintings on the walls. The dead lay beneath those platforms. Archaeologists looked here for a king’s palace and found none: the houses are alike. Perhaps that shows people could live long as equals; perhaps we are reading our own hopes into the past. Standing on the mound, look across the plain to distant Mount Hasan and ask: what makes a place a home?

What ties you to the place you live: its walls, its people or its memories?
