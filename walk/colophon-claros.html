Walk through Colophon and Claros

Xenophanes of Colophon · 6th–5th c. BC · İzmir (Menderes)

Xenophanes (c. 570–475 BC) was the first thinker to attack the habit of picturing the gods as human beings. When the Persians took Ionia he left Colophon, aged about twenty-five, and by his own account wandered the Greek world for sixty-seven years. In the city’s territory lay Claros, one of the great oracles of Apollo.

“Ethiopians say that their gods are snub-nosed and black; Thracians that theirs are blue-eyed and red-haired.”Xenophanes, fragment DK 21 B16, preserved by Clement of Alexandria; translation as in SEP

The acropolis of Colophon

On the hill above Değirmendere: walls and houses of the city Xenophanes left.

IdeaGods in our own image

Ethiopians picture their gods as black and snub-nosed, Thracians as blue-eyed and red-haired; and if horses and oxen had hands, says Xenophanes, they would draw gods shaped like horses and oxen. Our pictures of the divine tell us more about ourselves than about the gods. SEP ↗

Stop 2 of 3

The oracle of Apollo at Claros

A Doric temple, a great altar and the underground rooms where the prophet drank from the sacred spring.

IdeaOne god, not like mortals

Against Homer and Hesiod, who gave the gods theft, adultery and deceit, Xenophanes speaks of “one god greatest among gods and men, not at all like mortals in body or in thought”. This god stays in one place and shakes all things by the thought of his mind. SEP ↗

Stop 3 of 3

Notion

The harbour town of Claros: a theatre above the sea and a temple of Athena.

IdeaWisdom over victory

Cities honour Olympic champions with feasts and front seats, yet their strength does not make a city better governed. For Xenophanes “our wisdom” is worth more than the strength of men and horses. Philosophy becomes a claim about what a community ought to value. SEP ↗

Before you leave

Sit for a moment

In the sixth century BC Colophon was one of the rich cities of Ionia, famous for its cavalry and its luxury. Xenophanes was born here, left when the Persians came, and spent his life on the road, reciting his poems. Everywhere he went he saw the same thing: every people made its gods in its own likeness. Homer’s gods stole, lied and envied like human beings. Xenophanes thought this a disgrace. At Claros, the city’s oracle, people travelled far to ask Apollo about the future. Xenophanes proposed something else: the gods did not show us everything from the start; by searching, we find out better in time. Standing by the oracle’s temple, ask yourself: do you wait for an answer from a god, or go looking for it?

Think

When you picture what you hold highest, whose face does it wear?
