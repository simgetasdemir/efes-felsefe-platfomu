ḪATTUŠA

Walk through Hattusa

The Hittites and their laws, treaties and prayers · 17th–12th c. BC · Çorum (Boğazkale)

The Hittite capital Hattusa is an archive of thought before philosophy: thousands of clay tablets kept law codes, the oldest known surviving peace treaty and the prayers in which a king asked why a plague had come. It is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Stop 1 of 3

The Lion Gate

Two lions guarding the city’s southwest entrance.

IdeaCompensation instead of revenge

The Hittite laws survive on clay tablets, about two hundred clauses in all. Compared with the Code of Hammurabi their penalties are much milder: most offences call for compensation rather than retaliation. The code was copied for five hundred years and revised, more than once, to reduce its penalties. Wikipedia ↗

Stop 2 of 3

The Great Temple

The temple of the Storm God and the Sun Goddess, with its storerooms.

IdeaA promise of peace

Years after the battle of Kadesh, Hattusili III and Pharaoh Ramesses II agreed to a lasting peace and mutual aid. Both sides’ texts survive: the oldest record that enmity can be ended by an agreement. Wikipedia ↗

Stop 3 of 3

Yazılıkaya

An open-air sanctuary where the gods are carved into the rock.

IdeaThe land of a thousand gods

The Hittites called their land that of “the thousand gods of Hatti”: rather than destroy the gods of the peoples they conquered, they took them into their own pantheon. The procession of gods at Yazılıkaya is that gathering carved in stone. Wikipedia ↗

Before you leave

Sit for a moment

On this rocky slope, more than three thousand years ago, stood the capital of one of the first great empires of Anatolia. The scribes of Hattusa pressed cuneiform into clay tablets and kept them in the palace archives. The tablets held laws: whoever harmed another was usually punished with compensation, not revenge. Another tablet held the promise of peace between two kings after years of war. Another, the prayer of a king asking why the plague had come: my father broke his oath, he says, must I now pay for it? As you pass the Lion Gate, remember that a thousand years before the philosophers, people were already asking about justice, promises and guilt.
