Walk through Knidos

Eudoxus of Cnidus · 4th c. BC · Muğla (Datça)

Eudoxus (c. 390–340 BC), who worked at Plato’s Academy, was one of the greatest mathematicians of antiquity: the theory of proportion, the method of exhaustion and the model of nested spheres for the planets are his. He went home to Knidos and built an observatory. Praxiteles’ famous Aphrodite also stood in this city.

“Eudoxus thought pleasure was the good because he saw all things, both rational and irrational, aiming at it.”Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics X.2, 1172b9 (tr. W. D. Ross)

IdeaSaving the appearances

The planets seem to wander back and forth across the sky. Eudoxus explained each with nested spheres turning on different axes. Finding regular causes for a motion that looks irregular, it was astronomy’s first great theoretical model, and Aristotle built his cosmology on it. Wikipedia ↗

Before you leave

Sit for a moment

Knidos lies at the tip of the Datça peninsula, between two seas. The ancient city built two harbours here and grew famous for its merchants, sailors and physicians. Eudoxus left for Athens and Plato’s circle; after changing mathematics and astronomy he came home and built an observatory to watch the night sky. For him the wandering paths of the planets came from the regular turning of invisible spheres. In the same city stood Praxiteles’ Aphrodite; ancient writers tell of ships calling at Knidos just to see it. Sitting on the cape at dusk, look up and ask: is looking for order behind what seems irregular a habit, or a faith?

Think

What seems irregular in your life, and what order are you looking for behind it?
